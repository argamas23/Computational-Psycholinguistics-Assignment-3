## Content Words

Model 2 (surprisal) slightly outperforms Model 1 (frequency) in explaining mean reading time (lower AIC and higher R²).

This suggests that for content words, predictability (surprisal) from a language model might be a more informative predictor than raw frequency.

None of the predictors are statistically significant, but this may be due to sample size or noise in the data.

## Function Words

Model 4 (surprisal) also outperforms the frequency model (Model 3) with higher R² and lower AIC.

Interestingly, the coefficient for surprisal is negative, suggesting that less predictable function words are read faster — which may reflect habitual, automated processing of these words.

Still, predictors are not significant here either, likely due to the small sample size (only 27 function words).



The results provide preliminary support for the hypothesis that content and function words are processed differently. For both word types, models using language model surprisal offered slightly better fits than models using frequency. This effect was more pronounced for function words, where surprisal explained over 11% of the variance in mean RT, compared to only 4.8% by frequency.

Though the predictors were not statistically significant, the overall trend suggests that contextual predictability (as captured by language model probabilities) may be more informative than raw frequency—especially for content words that carry more semantic weight. Function words, by contrast, may be processed more automatically and exhibit different relationships with predictability.
